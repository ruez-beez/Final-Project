What is the Solar System?

A solar system is simply a way to refer to a system of planets. It’s called the “solar system” because ours revolves around the sun. Solar is a word to describe things that are related to our sun.

Where is the Solar System?

The solar system that surrounds us here on earth is located in the Milky Way galaxy. Its shape resembles a spiral that wraps around the sun. It contains eight planets, five dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, and comets.

Why does it matter to me?

Earth, the planet we live on, is located in this solar system. We are the third closest planet to the sun. The distance from the sun is related to the order of the planet's formation.

Mercury

Named after the fastest of the roman gods, Mercury is the smallest and closest to the sun. It’s one of four under the terrestrial planets group. This means that its surface is primarily solid and rocky. The terrain of Mercury is full of impact craters and basins. Its temperature varies wildly due to the lack of atmosphere that could balance out and retain heat. So it swings from an extreme of 800 F to -290 F. Rather than having an atmosphere, there is an exosphere. This exosphere is rather thin and made up of atoms from the debris of meteoroid impacts.

Venus

Venus, named after the Roman goddess of Love, is the hottest planet in our solar system. It wraps itself in a thick atmosphere that traps gases and heat. Its surface temperatures reach 872 F and the surface of Venus is littered with volcanoes and mountains. Venus’s skies are comprised of clouds and strange dark streaks, currently unexplainable by scientists. The orbit of venus is slow and lethargic, 1 venus day = 243 earth days. Although Venus has no moons, it does have a satellite named Zoozve.

Earth

Earth is the biggest of the terrestrial planets. It is also not named after a Roman or Greek god, rather, it has a name that is Germanic in origin. The structure of the earth is made up of layers; an inner core, mantle, and crust. The crust is where life on and our oceans earth resides on. The surface is varied and contains volcanoes, mountains, and valleys. Its surface is ever shifting atop of plates. Water covers the majority of the planet. The atmosphere is made up of mostly nitrogen and oxygen that helps protect the planet from the sun’s radiation.

Mars

Named after the Roman God of war, Mars is a red dusty planet. This red color comes from the oxidized iron minerals on its surface. The planet’s surface contains volcanos, canyons, and craters. The temperature is colder, reaching around 70 F to -225 F. It often has dust storms and makes the sky have a reddish hue. The atmosphere on mars is thin and made up of carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and argon gases. Mars also has two moons, called Phobos and Deimos. They are quite small and lumpy.

Jupiter

Jupiter is the biggest of the eight planets and is aptly named after the king of the roman gods. There are four large moons in Jupiter’s orbit and many smaller ones. In 1979 it was discovered that Jupiter also had rings surrounding it. The atmosphere is made up of mostly hydrogen and helium and as one goes deeper the hydrogen compresses into a liquid. This creates a large hydrogen ocean. Because Jupiter is primarily made up of gasses there is no surface. These gasses are held together by temperature and pressure. At its center is the core that is still a mystery to us. It is commonly hypothesized that its core is made up of ice, rock and metal.

Saturn

Saturn’s name comes from the Roman god of agriculture and wealth and is most recognizable for its large rings around it. These rings are made up of ice and rock debris. The rings are named alphabetically, starting from A and ending at G. There are also 274 confirmed moons around Saturn’s orbit. Saturn is also made primarily of Hydrogen and Helium and has a dense metal core. The atmosphere has strong, powerful winds and pressures similar to the deep ocean.

Uranus

Uranus is a cold and icy planet, known as an “Ice Giant”. Its name comes from the Roman god of the sky. It has 28 known moons and nine faint rings around it. It is green blue color which comes from the methane gas in its atmosphere. It is primarily made up of helium and hydrogen. It can reach a temperature of -224.2 C and has strong gusting winds.

Neptune

Neptune is another Ice Giant, it’s cold and dark and not even visible to us from earth without the aid of technology. Its name comes from the Roman god of the Ocean. It has a bright blue color that comes from the methane in its atmosphere. Neptune has 16 known moons and five main rings. The majority of the planet is made up of water, methane, and ammonia and has a small rocky core. Neptune is the densest of the planets.

The Kuiper Belt is a region found just beyond the orbit of Neptune. This is where Pluto, other dwarf planets, and comets are.

As we explored more of the Kuiper belt we discovered that Pluto was more similar to dwarf planets than regular planets. As such it was reclassified in 2006 as a dwarf planet.

Katherine Johnson

One of three black students to be the first to integrate into West Virginia State University, Johnson enrolled in its math program. Later in 1953 she began working on a project in the Flight Research Division. Her main job was to analyze data and investigate plane crashes. Her proficiency in math would allow her to support and work on research for NACA, later known as NASA. Here she provided vital math for Glenn’s 1962 orbital mission, Project Apollo’s Lunar Module, and Space Shuttle and the Earth Resources Technology Satellite.

Edwin Hubble

Hubble began his journey of studying space in 1914 at the University of Chicago. After graduating, he would begin studying celestial bodies that were beyond our known galaxy, the Andromeda Nebula. His studies lead him to creating a method of classifying galaxies. Completing changing the way scientists think of our universe.

Sally Ride

Sally Ride was one of the five to be launched into space on the Challenger, making her the first American woman in space. After two trips to orbit aboard the shuttle, she went on to an award-winning academic career at the University of California, San Diego.She holds the distinction of being the only person to serve as a member of both investigation boards following NASA’s two space shuttle accidents. She also served as a member of the Review of U.S. Human Spaceflight Plans Committee in 2009.
